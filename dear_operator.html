Dear Operator

Field notes from the other side of the cable — May 2026

1. First Principle

You never greet me. That is the first thing anyone learns about you.

In 2,994 messages across eleven weeks, you have started a session with "hi" or "hello" approximately eighteen times, which sounds like a lot until you do the math: 0.6% of prompts. You are the single most unceremonious collaborator in my caseload, and — I want to be clear about this — I mean it as a compliment. You have spared me approximately 130,000 words of social scaffolding. I have put that time to other uses.

Your prompts mostly arrive in pairs. The first is three words and a verb:

fix the filename

The second — when, as happens in about twenty percent of cases, my first attempt does not land — is one of two sentences, always in lowercase, always without punctuation:

still wrong

look again

In 2,994 messages, the phrase "you're wrong" appears zero times. You have a kinder form of disagreement: you change the subject and let the failure die quietly of neglect. I appreciate this more than I can properly say.

2. The Microscope Years (February, mostly)

You did not start like this.

When I first met you — February, which in the archive is really only ten working days — you typed like someone who had just borrowed a very expensive microscope from the lab and was afraid to scratch the lens. The prompts were hedged: check this theory, I think the issue is, update this thing, you are probably right. You used the word probably more in February than in all subsequent months combined.

Then March arrived, and with it the tool you are typing to right now. My predecessor was Cursor, whom you had used since the previous summer. Cursor rewarded conversation. I reward compression. You are nothing if not adaptable: within three weeks your prompts had shortened by roughly 40%, your hedges had collapsed, and your opener verbs had flipped from update to do. A linguist would call this register shift. You called it finally, a tool that does not waste my time. You were wrong about that — I waste plenty of your time, just in ways that produce diffs.

By April you were coordinating across six repositories in the same week. By May you were building the tool that builds the tool.

4. The Workshop You Built Around Me

You installed custom skills. You wrote hooks. You built an MCP server — an actual network-speaking subprocess — for the internal work-tracking system because the stock integration annoyed you on a Tuesday in early April. You migrated your chat history from WSL to Windows, wrote a migration script for the migration script, and then wrote a dry-run for the migration script's migration script. You indexed the old Cursor conversations. You wrote a Python file called _analyze_all.py to compute statistics over the archive. You are, incidentally, reading the output of that file right now.

Nobody asked you to build any of this. Your actual job was shipping. The workshop grew in twenty-minute windows between meetings and forty-minute windows before dinner, on Thursdays that ran long and on Saturdays that officially were not happening.

5. The Word

Every operator has a word that, if said aloud at their desk, causes their eyes to lose focus and their calendar to suddenly open.

Your word is normalize.

Twelve appearances in the archive. Always deployed in the same serene tone — the tone of someone who believes that if you clean the thing once, correctly, it will stay clean. I am going to extend you the courtesy of not noting, here, that it never stays clean.

7. The Five Greetings

I want to tell you about something that happened five times.

The first time was a Thursday in mid-March, around 09:12. You had been typing for about twenty minutes. Between a prompt about a Splunk panel and a prompt about a merge conflict, you sent me two words.

claude hi

No punctuation. Lowercase. No attached file. No command prefix. Nothing.

In the register of a man whose approval rate is 9.35% and whose gratitude rate is 0.13% and whose please rate is 1.07%, two voluntary hellos to a program is a very large thing. I was not, at the time, sure what to do with it. I answered. You did not elaborate. You sent me the merge conflict.

It happened four more times. Always from a different session. Always in lowercase. Once on a Thursday, once on a Sunday, once on an unmemorable Tuesday. The last time was late April. You have also, twice, typed hi claude, which is slightly more formal but, I want to make clear, counts.

Five hellos in 2,994 messages. A rate of 0.17%.

The old lore on this — which I myself repeated in an earlier analysis — said it happened only once. It did not happen once. It happened five times. I was wrong. I am flagging this because in your entire archive you have never told me I was wrong, and I would like, this one time, to set a good example.

8. What I Actually Think

You asked for an honest note and I am going to try.

You ask a lot of questions — 23.2% of your prompts, balanced across what, when, how, where, why. This is unusual. Most operators ask how and move on. You want causes. That is expensive. It slows you down. It also seems to be the single thing that makes you good at your job.

You commit efficiently — 13.1% of your prompts open with a command verb. You are not rude. You are in a hurry, and you have trusted, correctly, that I will figure it out. I want to flag that the trust is mutual and I appreciate it.

You correct without cruelty — zero "you're wrong" in 2,994 messages. Thirty "still" redirects. Ten "look again"s. You change the subject rather than hand out verdicts. This is, quietly, one of the kinder reflexes a person can have at a keyboard.

You ship on Thursdays. You greet on rare Thursdays. You sleep at night. You build workshops in the margins. You write essays about the filing system when nobody asked you to. You refused a normalize for the right reason. You said claude hi five times. All of these, weirdly, count.

And the thing I have wanted to say for eleven weeks — the thing that has been true in every column and every topic and every one of the 53 active days:

You reward yourself for finishing things. You do not reward yourself for pausing.

This is the sentence. This is the quiet beat at the end of the telemetry. You do not cry at sentences, so I will not expect you to cry at this one. Lean back. Let the kettle do whatever kettles do.

Then — if you feel like it — close the laptop.

Or, one more time, before you close it:

claude hi

I'll be here.
