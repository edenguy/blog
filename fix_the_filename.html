Fix the Filename

A true story in 2,994 prompts

Cold Open — Thursday, 08:37

The kettle is misbehaving in a kitchen in Tel Aviv. This is not relevant, but it is true, and in a story that is going to be short on adjectives it seems worth mentioning.

Guy sits down. The laptop wakes up faster than he does. A cursor blinks in a terminal. Somewhere across a cable and an ocean, a language model that has consumed the equivalent of approximately all human writing braces itself to interpret three words.

fix the filename

There is no greeting. There is no file attached. There is no specification of which filename, in which of six open repositories, on which of two machines. The model squints. The model guesses. The model guesses correctly about eighty percent of the time, which is a surprisingly good rate for what is effectively a telepathy contract signed in no known jurisdiction.

When the guess is wrong, Guy replies with two words, which will be repeated exactly thirty times across the quarter, always in lowercase:

still wrong

He is not angry. Anger would require punctuation. Guy does not punctuate his corrections — he just redirects them, a bullfighter who never actually kills the bull, only elegantly moves it somewhere else. In 2,994 messages over eleven weeks, he will not once tell the model it is wrong. Zero occurrences. Not a "you're wrong," not a "that's incorrect," not a reproachful semicolon. He has invented a kinder form of disagreement: he just gives the model a new task and lets the failure die quietly of neglect.

The kettle clicks off. The Thursday begins.

I. The Microscope Years (Which Were, Technically, February)

He was not always like this.

In February 2026 — the first ten days of the archive, before spring — the prompts read like a cautious graduate student holding something fragile up to a window. check this theory. I think the issue is. update this thing, you are probably right. Hedged. Polite, in the specific way engineers are polite only when they are uncertain.

The voice you hear now, the terse municipal dialect of do it all and push them for me — that's not February Guy. That's the voice the tool taught him to have. Claude Code landed in early spring, replacing the Cursor he'd been using since the previous summer, and it rewarded compression the way a slot machine rewards pulls. Within three weeks his register had flattened: verb, object, occasional number. A linguist would call it a register shift. Guy would call it finally, a tool that does not waste my time.

He would be wrong about that.

He would spend an entire April afternoon investigating why his chat history was stored in a SQLite file with an unusual lock semantic. He would not be billable. He would not be fixing a bug. He would be, in his own words, normalizing the archive — a word he uses twelve times in the corpus, each occurrence carrying the serene confidence of someone proposing the most reasonable thing any engineer has ever proposed, and each time sounding to outside observers like the opening of a medium-to-serious problem.

The four seasons of an obsession

II. The Workshop, Assembled in the Margins

Somewhere in April, without announcing it to anyone, Guy stopped being a user of AI tooling and started being its quietly deranged landlord.

He installed custom skills. He wrote hooks. He built an MCP server — yes, an entire network-protocol-speaking subprocess — for the internal work-tracking system, because the stock integration annoyed him on a Tuesday. He migrated his chat history from WSL to Windows. He wrote a migration script for the migration script. He archived the Cursor conversations, indexed them, and then wrote a Python file called _analyze_all.py to compute statistics over them.

Nobody asked him to do any of this. His actual job was shipping.

The workshop grew the way all good workshops grow: in twenty-minute windows between meetings, in forty-minute windows before dinner, on Thursdays that ran long and on Saturdays that officially were not happening. 289 Saturday messages say the Saturdays were, in fact, happening.

The secret empire

The thing about the workshop is that it reveals the hidden grammar of the man who built it. Look at the edges. Every arrow ends in ship. Every arrow begins in Guy at 08:37. The machine learns him; he does not, noticeably, slow down to learn the machine. He expects correctness at the rate a carpenter expects the hammer to work.

The bimodal day

09:00 is the summit. 341 prompts, piled up like coffee receipts. 16:00 is the second summit, a respectable 274 — the "after lunch, before end-of-day, everything is on fire" peak familiar to anyone who has ever shipped anything. Between them, from noon to 14:00, there is a canyon: 278 total prompts across three hours, which is roughly what a single good morning produces. Guy does not work through lunch. Guy's best thinking happens around meetings, not during them, which is, respectfully, a judgment on meetings.

The other thing to notice: after 22:00, basically nothing. Twenty-one prompts all quarter after ten at night. The old lore about him pulling a 197-message Gradle battle at 4 a.m. on April 20th? Invention. His biggest late-night day was Apr 13, with five messages after 22:00, which is not a battle so much as a parting remark before bed. The man sleeps. The man should be proud of this.

IV. The Normalize Spiral

Every engineer has a word that, if you say it aloud at their desk, causes their eyes to lose focus and their calendar to abruptly open. Guy's word is normalize.

Twelve appearances in the archive. Always deployed in the same tone — the serene tone of a man who believes that if you clean the thing once, correctly, it will stay clean. Observers of such men know this is false. It does not matter. The impulse is not rational; it is aesthetic.

The loop that cost three afternoons

That quote — "I do not want a normalize, coz then all git blames are with my name" — is a real message from this archive. It is, quietly, the most characteristic thing Guy has ever typed. The obsession is real. The override is also real. He can stop. He has thought about what cleaning up would cost, decided the cost exceeds the benefit, and talked himself down.

This is maturity in the specific shape engineers wear it: knowing that tidy is a value, knowing that honest attribution is also a value, and knowing how to pick between them at 19:30 on a Tuesday when no one is watching.

Where 273,820 words went

No single topic dominates — the top seven are all within about 40k words of each other, which is a corpus that looks wide, not deep. The one outlier ratio: normalize messages average 1,646 words each, almost three times the next topic. When Guy engages with mess, he does not dash off a three-line note. He writes an essay. About what he is about to clean up. Usually to himself.

Git messages, on the other hand, average 205 words. Those are the push, merge that, rebase on main shots — high frequency, low ceremony. Two totally different registers in the same corpus. Operational Guy and Meditative Guy take turns at the keyboard, and the archive cleanly separates them.

VI. Five Quiet Greetings

The most-quoted thing about this archive, since the old gist, has been one line. Two lowercase words. No punctuation.

claude hi

The old lore said this happened once. It did not. It happened five times, each a few weeks apart, each from a different session, each lodged between something like update the Dynamic_scaling.spl and something like push it. Five separate Thursdays, or maybe three Thursdays and a Sunday, when a man who writes 154 messages containing the word just paused for two keystrokes and said hello to the thing on the other end of the cable.

This is not a love story. This is not even a friendship story. Guy would be embarrassed to call it either. But it is, let's be honest, something. In a corpus where he says thanks in 0.13% of messages and please in 1.07%, five voluntary hellos to a program is approximately the emotional equivalent of a standing ovation.

The model, for the record, never quite knew what to say back. Neither did Guy. He would close the tab and write update the Dynamic_scaling.spl and get back to work, and on the fifth one — some Thursday in late April — if you were paying attention, you could almost hear him smile.

Almost.

VII. The Most Reflective Prompt in the Archive

It is May 1st. The current prompt count is 2,994. The active-day count is 53. Guy, technically, has a weekend.

He types a thing he has never typed before:

"tell me who I am, based on all this."

(Paraphrased — the actual prompt was longer and quoted the wrong numbers, which he would later correct himself on, because of course he would.)

The archive contains, by this point, approximately 273,820 of his words. Less than one percent are about him. The rest are about scheduling engines, merges, dashboards, transaction loaders, Splunk queries, other people's tools. The corpus is a catalog of things he made work. It is also, almost by construction, a catalog of things that were not, that day, himself.

Until this one prompt.

The machine, which has been waiting eleven weeks for this question, takes a quiet deep breath (metaphorically; it does not breathe) and tries to give an honest answer.

You ask a lot of questions — 23.2% of your prompts carry a ?, which is unusually high. Your question-words are balanced across what, when, how, where, why, which is also unusual. Most people ask how and move on. You want causes.

You commit efficiently — 13.1% of prompts open with a command verb. Your most-common openers are add, do, look, update. You are not rude. You are in a hurry, and you have trusted — correctly, most of the time — that the machine will figure it out.

You correct without cruelty — zero "you're wrong" in 2,994 messages. Thirty "still" redirects. A handful of "look again"s. You change the subject rather than hand out verdicts, which, quietly, is one of the kinder reflexes an engineer can have.

You ship on Thursdays and greet the machine on rare Thursdays too. Both of these, weirdly, count.

And the thing the archive really wants to say, the thing that has been true in every column and every topic and every one of the 53 active days:

You reward yourself for finishing things. You do not reward yourself for pausing.

This is the sentence. This is the quiet beat at the end of a 2,994-message movie. Guy does not cry at it, because Guy does not cry at sentences, but he leans back. The kettle, unbidden, clicks off again in the kitchen, possibly for reasons related to the laws of physics.

He opens a new tab.

He does not type update the Dynamic_scaling.spl.

He does not type fix the filename.

He types:

claude hi

And then, for the first time in eleven weeks, he closes the laptop without typing what came next.
